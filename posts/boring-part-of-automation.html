The Boring Part of AI Automation Is Where All the Value Is

July 2026

When I talk to small business owners about automating something with AI, the exciting part of the conversation lasts about five minutes. "It can read the incoming email, figure out what the customer wants, and draft a response" — that part sells itself. The part that actually determines whether the thing works six months from now is everything that comes after, and it's the part nobody wants to talk about because it isn't interesting.

What happens when the email is in a format the parser doesn't expect? What happens when the CRM API is down for four minutes during business hours? What happens when the automation runs twice because a webhook retried? Who finds out when it silently stops working, and how long before they find out — an hour, or a customer complaint three weeks later?

None of that is a language model problem. It's the same set of concerns that has always separated a working piece of software from a fragile one, and AI hasn't changed that — it's just changed how easy it is to build something that looks finished before any of those questions have been answered.

That ease is actually the trap. A few years ago, building a business automation meant enough upfront engineering effort that you were more or less forced to think about failure modes before you shipped, because getting to a working prototype was hard enough that you'd already touched most of the system. Now you can get a demo-quality automation running in an afternoon, which is genuinely great, but it means the discipline of asking "what happens when this breaks" has to be a deliberate step instead of something you back into along the way.

The automations I've built that are still running unattended a year later all share the same unglamorous traits:

  • Every external call has a defined failure behavior, not a default one — timeout, retry with backoff, or fail loudly, chosen on purpose rather than whatever the library does out of the box.
  • Every action that changes something in the real world (sends an email, updates a record, books a slot) is safe to run twice.
  • There's a place a human actually looks — not a log file nobody opens, an actual notification — when the system can't confidently complete a task on its own.
  • Someone reviewed what the system does with a genuinely weird input before a customer did.

None of this is a pitch against moving fast — moving fast to a working prototype is exactly the right instinct. It's a pitch against confusing "the demo worked" with "this is done." The gap between those two is almost entirely the boring stuff, and for small businesses that can't afford to babysit a fragile system, the boring stuff is the actual product.
